The Vietnamese Famine

1945: STORIES OF HUNGER

1945: STORIES OF HUNGER

From the winter of 1944 to the summer of 1945, hunger swept across northern and north-central Vietnam and took hundreds of thousands of lives, by some estimates as many as two million, amid war, seized rice stores and failed harvests. To Minh Thuyet was one of the very few lucky enough to survive, and this story follows his memories, from the hungriest days and the people who fell by the roadside, until the famine receded and left behind losses that nothing could repay. Then history turned: the August Revolution broke out, and on 2 September 1945, at Ba Dinh Square, President Ho Chi Minh read the Declaration of Independence. Behind the numbers are individual lives, and these pages are here so that they are remembered.

• • •

1944: ORDINARY DAYS

1944

Thai Binh

The Thuyet family lived an ordinary life in a small village in Thai Binh. Every morning, the parents went to work in the fields while the six children stayed at home. By the afternoon, everyone returned and gathered together again. Their meals were simple, but there was still enough for the family to sit together. One day was much like the next, and no one imagined that their lives would soon change.

One afternoon, Thuyet’s mother hurried home from the fields, her face filled with fear. Looking at her eldest daughter, she began to cry: “Kheo ca lang chet doi mat thoi con oi” – “Perhaps the whole village will starve to death, my child.” From that moment, another life began.

Late 1944 – Early 1945: HUNGER BEGINS

1944

Late 1944

The food on the family’s table gradually became scarcer. In the fields of Thuong village and the surrounding villages, the rice turned white like the wings of storks, with hardly any full grains to be found. No one knew what had happened to the crops. People could only say that it was “something brought by the heavens.” When they no longer had enough rice to pay their rent, villagers went to wealthy households to borrow food, but they were refused. The Thuyet family’s own supply of rice gradually ran out.

Hunger
Begins

1945

Early in the year

Thuyet’s mother had to carry household belongings, including old bowls and dishes, to the landlord Ly Sach’s house to exchange for a few kilograms of rice. The whole family depended on this small amount of rice to survive from one day to the next. But eventually, there was nothing left to sell or exchange. Some days, she returned home in the rain carrying her empty basket, with nothing to feed her six children.

Early 1945: NOTHING LEFT
TO EAT

1945

Early 1945

When there was nothing left to sell, people began eating whatever they could find. Ten-year-old Thuyet still remembered villagers going into the fields to collect water spinach, purslane and old cabbage leaves. Dogs and cats were killed because people could no longer afford to feed them. Some families even sold their buffaloes cheaply, but when no one would buy them, they had to slaughter them and eat the meat little by little. When the plants and vegetables were gone, people caught rats in the fields and dug for wild roots, taro and banana stems to make food.

• • •

Nothing Left
to Eat

1945

The village

Hunger gradually transformed the entire village. Families that still had banana or papaya trees had to guard them at night for fear of theft. People fought over a single banana plant. Then the last sources of food disappeared, and people began to die. Starving bodies lay along the village roads, transforming what had once been an ordinary village into a place where survival itself had become uncertain.

March 1945: LEAVING HOME

1945

March

When there was nothing left to eat in the village, people began leaving their homes in search of a chance to survive. In March 1945, ten-year-old To Minh Thuyet joined his parents and a large group of people travelling from Thai Binh towards Ha Noi. Even years later, he could still remember the journey. Mothers and children supported one another as they walked among crowds of starving people; many were barely able to walk, gradually collapsing along the road.

They were not leaving home in search of a better life. They were leaving because staying meant starving.

March–April 1945: HA NOI

1945

March – April

When the family reached Ha Noi, they lived underneath Long Bien Bridge. Thuyet’s father worked as a rickshaw driver, but there were few passengers, so the family continued to live from one uncertain meal to the next. In the city, farmers from surrounding areas continued to flood into the streets looking for food. Ha Noi was no longer a place where survival was easy. The streets were filled with starving people, and the bodies of those who had died from hunger could be seen everywhere.

There were so many bodies that carts could no longer carry them away for burial, and trucks had to be used instead. For the Thuyet family, the city was not the end of the famine. It was simply another place where they had to fight to survive.

Ha Noi

April 1945: FATHER NEVER RETURNED

1945

April

While the family remained in Ha Noi, Thuyet’s parents decided that his father should return to their village to sell the family’s wooden house frame, hoping to obtain some money and rice. Before he left, his mother gave him a small handful of rice, no bigger than a child’s fist. He returned to the village while the children stayed behind in Ha Noi, waiting for him.

• • •

Father Never
Returned

1945

April

But he never came back.

The family eventually received news that he had died from starvation while waiting for someone to buy the house. No one bought it. A father had left to find a way to save his family, but in the end, he could not survive himself.

After the Famine: SURVIVAL

1945

After the famine

The Thuyet family was fortunate to find another way to survive. His eldest sister went to work as a wet nurse for a wealthy family, bringing leftover food home to her mother and younger siblings. Those small portions of food helped the family survive the final days of the famine.

Survival

1945

After the famine

But the story of the Thuyet family was only one of thousands of stories from the famine. Some families lost their fathers, mothers or children. Some lost everyone. And many of those stories had no one left alive to tell them.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

But Thuyet’s family was only one story.

The story of the Thuyet family opens up a much larger picture. The famine did not occur in just one village; it spread across Northern and North-Central Vietnam. Crop failures, natural disasters, war, food policies, and disruptions to transportation and rice distribution all contributed to the crisis. While some areas in the South still had rice supplies, many areas in the North faced severe food shortages.

As food supplies ran out, large numbers of people were forced to leave their homes and travel towards cities such as Ha Noi in search of food, work and relief. By early 1945, starvation had become a widespread crisis affecting both rural communities and urban areas.

CONSEQUENCES

The famine left losses that went far beyond a number of deaths. Thousands of families lost loved ones, communities were deeply affected, and many people were forced to leave their homes. The famine also contributed to growing social dissatisfaction and became an important part of the historical context leading to the August Revolution of 1945.

Yet when we look back at history, the famine is often remembered through numbers, dates and events. The story of To Minh Thuyet allows us to see what existed behind those numbers: a family, a mother, a child, a father who never returned — and thousands of other families who experienced similar suffering.
